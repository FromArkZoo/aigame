Group observation: the three non-CA games (D, G, B — plus F, whose capture rule never fires)
occupy the top four places; the three actor-perspective CA siblings (C, E, A) occupy the
bottom three. The dividing line is human legibility of consequences: in D/G/B/F every
decisive event in my lines was plannable in advance; in C/E the decisive cascades were only
findable by sweeping candidates through the engine; in A the decisive event is at ply 1.

## Most want to play again

**Game D — by 0.2 Overall points over G** (4.5 vs 4.3). The margin is small but the reason
is not: D is the game where I most often discovered that my instinct was wrong for a
reason I could articulate afterwards (fake threats, poisoned blocks, corner fortresses),
which is the property I'd seek in repeated play. G I would also happily replay, but it
leans on skills Go already trains; D's skills exist nowhere else.

## The single most differentiating mechanic of the top-ranked game

The **enemy-adjacency placement constraint interacting with a connection win**: in D you
may only place next to opponent stones, so you literally cannot approach your own goal
without enemy "escorts," win completions are usually delivered by custodian flips (which
admit no interposition), and defensive blocks can be fatal because they legalize the
opponent's next placement (Line 1's losing move). Every other game in the set lets you
develop your own position by your own choice — free placement (G, B, A) or own-adjacency
growth (F, C, E). Only D makes the opponent's stones the enabling resource for your own
plan, and that single inversion generates its entire distinctive strategy layer
(legality starvation, fake threats, legality fuel) — the clearest case in this set of one
rule producing a genuinely new game rather than recombining familiar experiences.
