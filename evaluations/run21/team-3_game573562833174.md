## Phase 2 — Strategic Play

Sequence: `36,0,37,1,38,2,39,3,40,4,41,5,42,6,43,7,44` → **Done=True Winner=1 at ply 17.** P1 walks a straight horizontal row (y=4) in 9 stones; P2 (playing row y=0 = wrong axis) never threatens. Confirms the first-mover straight-line rush wins against a passive/mis-aimed P2.

### Game 2 — P1 flips a mid-line blocker (capture as offence)
Sequence: `36,9,37,18,38,27,39,40,41` → P1 has (3,4); P2 blocks at (4,4)=40; **P1 plays (5,4)=41, bracketing and flipping (4,4) to P1** (P1 4→6 pieces). A single interposed blocker is *useless* — P1 flips it and the row reconnects. **This is the structural improvement over R8 Connection Go: blocking is no longer a clean defence.**

### Game 3 — P2 cuts P1's chain, P1 re-flips (cut / counter-cut)
Sequence: `36,29,37,4,38,47,46,5,48`:
- P1 builds (0,4),(1,4),(2,4). **P2 vertically brackets (2,4) with (2,3)=29 and (2,5)=47 → flips (2,4) to P2** — P1's row is **cut** at x=2, and P2 gains a stone for its own column (double duty).
- **P1 re-brackets:** (1,5)=46 and (3,5)=48 bracket P2's (2,5)=47 → flips it back to P1 (P1 3→5). The cut stone changes hands again.

### Game 4 — Contested crossing cell (the crux)
Sequence: `36,4,37,13,38,22,39,31,40,49`: P1 rushes row y=4, P2 rushes column x=4; they cross at (4,4)=40. P1 takes (4,4) on ply 9; **P2 immediately flips it back with the vertical bracket (4,3)=31 + (4,5)=49 (ply 10)** — one move that *simultaneously cuts P1's row and completes part of P2's column.* The crossing cell of two perpendicular lines is genuinely fought over.

### Strategy guides
**P1 (offence, horizontal):** rush a row, but keep your stones un-bracketable vertically (avoid lone stones a column-pair can flip); flip P2's interposed blockers. Open near centre to keep two parallel rows live as a backup.
**P2 (defence + own vertical):** don't interpose single blockers (they get flipped) — **cut P1's row by vertically bracketing a key stone**, ideally at the crossing cell where the flip also advances your column. Race your own column meanwhile.

## Phase 3 — Joint Strategic Analysis

**Distinct viable strategies?** Yes for the *design*: rush vs cut/counter-cut, with the contested crossing cells as the battleground. The custodian capture makes the connection race genuinely two-sided.
**Counter-play.** **Real** — the cut/recut battle (Games 3–4) is the deepest tactical interaction in the entire slate. A blocker can be flipped; a cut can be re-flipped; the crossing cell swings both connections.
**Short-term vs long-term.** Genuine 2–3-ply tactical sequences (bracket → flip → re-bracket). The connection goal also imposes a board-spanning plan. This is the longest planning horizon in the slate.
**Emergent concepts.** **Double-duty captures** (a flip both cuts the opponent and builds your own connection), **contested crossing cells**, cut/reconnect, placement-safety. The richest emergent set in the slate — *if* you judge the design rather than the (failed) policy.
**Does grid matter?** The flat 9×9 with uniform orthogonal brackets is the right stage for custodian-cut connection play; no holes to break lines. The board (81 cells, 9-long connections) is bigger and more contested than R8's 8×8 — the R8-replay "too small for ladders" concern is *partially* eased but not removed (still no diagonal bridges; cuts, not ladders, carry the depth).
**Does the propagation kernel matter?** **No — it is `none`.** And that absence is the project-level failure: with no influence field, PPO has only the sparse win/loss signal and **cannot learn the game (GE ~0).** The design depth exists but is invisible to the trainer.
**Capture contribution.** **Central and live** — custodian flips are the entire tactical engine. Unlike R8 (surround inert) and the menger/carpet games (outnumber inert), captures here are the point.
**First-mover / seat balance.** **G3 FAIL — RUSH-BROKEN, mirror seat bias 0.50 at every komi; pie cannot fix it** (perpendicular axes on a symmetric board are structurally P1-favoured by tempo). My play nuances this: the 0.50 comes from capture-blind sampled/greedy policies; **capture-aware play gives P2 real cutting counterplay** (Games 3–4), so for skilled agents it is more contested than 0.50 implies — but P1's tempo + symmetry still favour P1. Balance is the killer flaw.

## Phase 4 — Novelty Adversary (mandatory)

**Adversary case.**
(a) **Connection-win across perpendicular faces is exactly Hex/Y's defining structure** — a 60-year-studied family. The win-condition is **not** novel.
(b) **Custodian capture = Othello/Reversi flipping**, here used to cut/reconnect a connection path.
(c) "Reversi-flip cutting a Hex-style connection race on a square grid" has no exact published analogue — the *combination* (flip-to-cut on a connection path) is the genuine new piece, and it is what R8 Connection Go lacked (its surround capture never fired).
(d) Flat 9×9, 4-connected — no diagonal bridges, so no true Hex ladders; the depth comes from cuts, not bridges. Square-grid connection games are known to be shallower than hex-grid Hex, but the live custodian capture adds a dimension Hex lacks.
(e) Expert transfer: a Hex + Othello player understands the goal instantly and the cut tactic in ~10 min; the double-duty-flip-at-the-crossing-cell is the one wrinkle to learn.

**Closest known-game analogue:** Hex/Y connection race on a square grid, with Othello-style custodian captures used to cut and reconnect paths — no exact published match.
**Comparison to R8 Connection Go (replay anchor 4.10; my team-3 R8 verdict 3.0).** **Same family, but materially better:** R8's surround captures were inert, so blocking had a clean structural advantage and the game reduced to "Hex on a worse grid." **Here custodian captures actually fire and create real cut/counter-cut battles** — the central flaw I docked R8 for is fixed. The board is also larger (9×9 vs 8×8). Against that: R8 trained (GE 0.386); this does not train at all (GE ~0).
**Comparison to R19/R20 best.** More tactical liveness in its capture rule than the R20 menger/carpet champions (whose captures were inert), but worse balance and untrainable.

**Novelty score (post-adversary):** **4/10.** Above re-skins (3) because flip-to-cut on a connection path is a real, interacting combination R8 lacked. Below genuinely-new (8) because connection-win is Hex's signature and the captures are Othello's. Anchor: R8 4.10, R19 top 4.8/5.0.
