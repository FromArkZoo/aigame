### Game 2 — Custodian CUTS a connection path (the decisive finding)

P2 has genuine agency — unlike R8 Connection Go, where capture never fired and P1 always won.

### Game 3 — Edge-immunity vs block-and-cut (the real sub-game)
- Edge immunity: P1 rushes row y=0 (top edge) → **uncuttable** (edge stones have no cell beyond the border to bracket against) → P1 wins ply 17. So the *optimal* P1 line is an edge rush, which custodian cannot touch.
- Block-and-cut counter: P2 plays `(4,0)` early to **block P1's edge row**, forcing P1 to detour to `(4,1)`; P2 then brackets `(3,1)+(5,1)` → **flips the detoured (4,1)**. So blocking the edge forces P1 off it, where stones become cuttable. A real attack/defence sub-game.
- Pie: P1 `40`, P2 `82` → swap flips P1's opening stone; clean seat transfer.

### Strategy guides
**P1 (offence):** Rush a connection along an **edge** (y=0 or y=8) where path stones are uncuttable. If P2 blocks the edge, detour minimally and defend the detour cell's brackets (thicken) before P2 can flip it.
**P2 (defence + own race):** Block P1's edge to force a cuttable detour, then **custodian-flip the detour stone** — this both breaks P1's path and builds your perpendicular column. You can win (verified), but the board is small and sharp.

## Phase 3 — Joint Strategic Analysis

**Distinct viable strategies?** Yes, more than any other slate game: edge-rush vs interior-rush (cuttable) for P1; block-to-force-detour + cut for P2. The custodian cut creates a genuine attack/defence interplay around the connection.
**Counter-play.** **Real and firing** — the defender can cut path stones (interior via vertical bracket, detours via horizontal bracket) and the cut doubles as offence. This is the slate's only game with live, consequential counter-play.
**Short-term vs long-term.** ~16–17-ply games on a small board → the R8 "too small for ladders" limit caps the horizon. Cuts are tactical, not deeply strategic, because there isn't room for long ladders/feints.
**Emergent concepts.** Path-cutting (break + steal), edge-immunity, block-forces-detour, contested shared cells. These are genuine emergent tactics from custodian + connection.
**Does the grid matter?** It's flat (no substrate). 9×9 is small — the size is a *limit*, not an asset (R8 replay concern confirmed).
**Does the kernel matter?** No — propagation is none (dead). The game is pure capture + connection; influence never enters win logic (the very limitation the R8-replay teams raised).
**Capture contribution.** **The highest in the slate** — custodian fires constantly and is decisive (it cuts paths and wins races). This is the crucial upgrade over R8 Connection Go (whose surround never fired).
**Seat balance.** **RUSH-BROKEN (G3 FAIL).** Mirror seat bias 0.50 at every komi — perpendicular win-axes on a symmetric board with first-move tempo. Pie transfers one stone but the bias is structural; komi cannot balance it. In careful play P2 can win (I did), but the *metric* balance is broken and play is sharp/swingy.

**Adversary case.** This is R8 Connection Go (Hex-on-squares) re-built with a *firing* custodian capture and no influence.
(a) Win = **connection** = Hex/Bridg-It's defining mechanic; asymmetric face goals on a square grid = square-grid Hex.
(b) Capture = **custodian/Othello**, and unlike R8's surround it **fires and cuts paths** — this is the irreducible new piece vs R8.
(c) "connection + custodian-cut + (dead influence)" — the connection+capture *interaction* (cut breaks path and steals for the perpendicular goal) is genuinely fresh; I know of no published connection game where the capture both cuts the opponent's path and advances yours.
(d) 9×9 flat grid = no substrate novelty, and small enough to cap depth.
(e) Expert-transfer: a Hex+Othello player gets the idea in ~10 min, but the **cut-as-dual-purpose** is a real new wrinkle to internalise.

**Closest known-game analogue:** square-grid Hex / Bridg-It with an Othello-style cutting capture layered on.
**Comparison to R8 Connection Go (replay anchor 4.10; my own team-5 R8 verdict 3.0).** **Same family, materially better mechanic.** R8's surround capture never fired (inert decoration); here custodian **fires and is decisive**, giving the defender real agency and the second player a path to win. On mechanic-liveness and counter-play this is clearly ahead of R8. It is still held back by the same small-board limit, the dead influence layer, and (worse than R8) a structurally rush-broken balance.
**Comparison to R19/R20:** richer *interaction* than the R20 influence-races (real cuts vs paper captures), but its balance (rush-broken) and unlearnability (GE~0) are worse. Thinner than R19's surround top (5.0).

**Novelty score (post-adversary):** 4.0/10. The connection + firing-custodian-cut combination is the freshest, most interactive design in the slate — above rule-combination novelty — but below genuinely-new because the backbone is square-grid Hex and the substrate adds nothing. ### CLOSEST KNOWN-GAME ANALOG
Square-grid Hex / Bridg-It with an Othello-style cutting capture — inside the corpus, R8 Connection Go but with a capture that actually fires.

### KILLER FLAWS
- **RUSH-BROKEN balance** — perpendicular goals on a symmetric board; first mover's edge-rush is structurally favoured, komi cannot balance it.
- **Dead influence layer + vestigial threshold** — half the rule blob is inert (the substrate/representation limit the R8-replay teams raised).
- **9×9 is too small** — no room for the ladders/feints that make Hex deep; the cut sub-game is tactical, not strategic.
- **PPO-unlearnable (GE≈0)** — even if the design has depth, the engine's agents can't access it; an agent-fitness pipeline will always discard it.

### BEST QUALITY
The **custodian cut as a dual-purpose move**: flipping an enemy path stone simultaneously breaks their connection and extends your own perpendicular one. This is the single most interactive mechanic in the slate and the clearest evidence that "restore connection-win" (G6) can produce a real game — the capture is no longer inert decoration as it was in R8.

### GRID STRUCTURAL CONTRIBUTION
Negative — flat 9×9 adds no structure and is small enough to cap depth. A connection+cut game wants a larger and/or hex board (degree-6, longer diameter) for the cut/ladder interplay to breathe; the grid is the wrong substrate, exactly as the R8 replay warned.

### IMPROVEMENT IDEAS
**Single best change:** move this design to a **larger hex board (e.g. 13×13, degree-6) with the pie rule doing real work** — the connection + firing-custodian-cut interaction is the slate's best idea but needs room (and a balance-able geometry) to become deep; the current grid-9 rush-broken implementation strangles it.
Secondary:
- Re-introduce influence *into the win logic* (e.g. connection OR influence-threshold) so the dead propagation layer earns its place and PPO gets a gradient to learn from — directly addressing the GE≈0 learnability failure.
- For G6/fitness: this is the proof that GE (a PPO-learnability proxy) and design-depth diverge — GE≈0 yet this is the most interactive design I evaluated. A connection-relevant board + influence-in-win-logic + a learnability-independent depth metric (per the decision rule's PIVOT branch) is the right response.
