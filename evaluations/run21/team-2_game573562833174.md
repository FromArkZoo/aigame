## Phase 3 — Joint Strategic Analysis

**Distinct viable strategies?** Yes — two genuine archetypes: **race your own connection** vs **cut+build through the opponent's line.** This is the only slate game with more than one real plan.
**Counter-play.** Real and bidirectional: every cut stone is also a builder (R8 dual-purpose); defenders reinforce flanks or detour. Custodian-1 is a *sharper* cutting tool than R8's Go-liberty capture (it fires with 2 stones, no liberty-counting), so the cut/defend battle is more active than R8's.
**Short-term vs long-term.** Horizon ~5–8 moves (read opponent's axis, contest crossings, plan a detour). Games ~17 plies under sharp play — short, but with real tactical depth, the longest *effective* planning horizon in the slate.
**Emergent concepts observed.** Cut+build dual-purpose stones; the connection-is-also-a-block (Hex) property; perpendicular custodian cuts; crossing-cell control; detour/reroute around a cut.
**Does grid matter?** The flat 9×9 is constraint-only, and at this size (R8's 8×8 class) the R8-replay "too small for ladders/territory" concern applies — the strategic horizon is capped by the board. 9×9 is marginally better than R8's 8×8 but still small.
**Does the propagation kernel matter?** No — propagation is `none`. Pure topological connection + capture. (Cleanly so; no decorative influence field, unlike R8 where influence was inert decoration. Here it's honestly absent.)
**Capture contribution.** **Central** — custodian cutting is the engine of the contest. Unlike R8 (where Go-liberty captures rarely fired against chains), custodian-1 reliably cuts a connection line at its tip. This is the design's best feature.
**First-mover / seat balance.** **Structurally broken at the naive level** (mirror bias 0.50; perpendicular symmetric axes; komi can't fix it; pie can't either). BUT skilled play is contested (both seats won in my games) — the "rush-broken" verdict describes greedy/PPO-level play, not the design's skilled ceiling. This is the crux of the G6 finding.
